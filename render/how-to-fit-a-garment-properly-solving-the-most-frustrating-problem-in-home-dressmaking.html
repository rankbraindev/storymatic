How to Fit a Garment Properly: Solving the Most Frustrating Problem in Home Dressmaking

There is a moment that almost every home dressmaker knows intimately. You have spent hours carefully cutting your fabric, following every instruction, pressing every seam — and then you try the finished garment on, only to find it pulls across the shoulders, bags at the waist, or gaps at the back. The fabric does not lie the way it did in the pattern illustration. The silhouette that looked so effortless on the line drawing looks nothing like that in the mirror. It is, without question, the most demoralising experience the craft has to offer.

The good news is that poor fit is almost never the result of a lack of skill. It is the result of a mismatch — between a standardised pattern and a body that does not conform to any standard. Every person who has ever sewn a garment has encountered this, from absolute beginners to seasoned dressmakers. The difference between those who push through and those who quietly put their machine away is simply knowing what to do about it. This guide is about exactly that.

Why Fit Is the Foundation of Every Garment Worth Wearing

There is a reason why a bespoke suit commands such reverence, or why a vintage dress bought from a market stall can look a million times better than something pulled off a high-street rail — and it has nothing to do with the fabric, the label, or even the construction quality. It is fit. A garment that fits well seems to belong on the body. It moves with you rather than against you. It flatters not because it hides anything, but because it works with your actual shape rather than imposing a standardised one upon it.

This matters even more in home dressmaking than it does in ready-to-wear clothing, and for a subtle reason. When you buy something from a shop that fits imperfectly, you tend to blame yourself — too tall, too short, the wrong proportions. When you make something yourself and it does not fit, it feels like a failure of the craft. That sting is unfair and inaccurate, but it is real, and it stops a great many people from persevering.

The truth is that fit problems in home dressmaking are almost always structural rather than personal. Pattern companies draft their designs to a set of standardised measurements that represent no single person in particular. They are averages, and like all averages, they describe a theoretical body rather than a real one. Add to this the fact that different pattern publishers use slightly different size charts, and that ease — the extra room built into a pattern beyond your actual measurements — varies enormously depending on the style and the brand, and you begin to understand why your finished garment rarely looks the way the packet promised.

Understanding this shifts the entire conversation. Fit is not something that happens to you; it is something you learn to control. A well-fitted handmade garment is not the product of luck or of having the "right" body — it is the result of understanding how a flat piece of paper becomes a three-dimensional shape, and knowing where to intervene when the translation goes wrong. Every section of this guide is aimed at giving you that understanding, from taking your first measurements through to making adjustments on the finished cloth.

Understanding Your Measurements and How They Map to Pattern Sizing

If fitting problems begin anywhere, they begin here — at the very first step, before a single piece of fabric has been cut. Most people either skip taking their measurements altogether, relying on a vague sense of what size they usually buy, or they take them once, years ago, and assume nothing has changed. Neither approach serves you well at the cutting table.

Taking Your Measurements Accurately

Measuring yourself properly is a small investment of time that pays back enormously across every project you make. You will need a dressmaker's tape measure, fitted clothing or just your underwear, and ideally a second person to help — measuring your own back length or high bust is genuinely awkward to do solo. If you must measure yourself, stand in front of a mirror so you can check that the tape is sitting level.

The key measurements every dressmaker should record are:

  • Bust: measured around the fullest part of the chest, with the tape parallel to the floor
  • High bust: measured above the bust, under the arms and across the upper chest — useful for choosing bodice sizes
  • Waist: measured at the narrowest point, usually a few centimetres above the navel
  • Hips: measured around the fullest part, typically 20–23cm below the natural waist
  • Back length: from the prominent bone at the base of the neck to the natural waist
  • Shoulder width: from shoulder point to shoulder point across the back
  • Sleeve length: from shoulder point to wrist, with the arm slightly bent

Write these down and keep them somewhere accessible. Update them at least once a year, or whenever you notice that recent makes are fitting differently than expected.

How Pattern Sizing Actually Works

Here is where many sewers come unstuck. Pattern sizes bear almost no relationship to ready-to-wear clothing sizes. A person who wears a size 12 in most high-street shops may find themselves cutting a size 16 or 18 from a sewing pattern — and this is not a problem with the pattern or with them. It is simply a different numbering convention, one that has remained closer to older sizing standards while retail clothing sizes have shifted dramatically over the decades.

The only number that matters when selecting a pattern size is your body measurement, compared directly against the pattern's size chart. Every reputable pattern publisher includes one, and it is the single most important piece of information on the packaging. Always measure yourself against the chart rather than assuming your size.

It is also worth knowing that most pattern companies recommend choosing your size based on your bust measurement for tops and dresses, and your hip measurement for skirts and trousers. If your measurements place you in different sizes for different parts of your body — which is extremely common — you will need to grade between sizes, a technique covered later in this guide.

The Role of Ease

Beyond your body measurements, every pattern includes ease — additional room built into the design beyond your actual size. Ease is what allows you to move, breathe, and sit down without splitting a seam. It comes in two forms: wearing ease, which is the practical minimum required for comfort and movement, and design ease, which is a deliberate stylistic choice that creates the intended silhouette.

A fitted bodice might include just 2–3cm of ease across the bust. A relaxed, oversized top might include 15cm or more. Neither is right or wrong — they produce different looks and different fits. The pattern envelope will usually describe the finished garment measurements separately from the size chart, and comparing these to your own measurements is one of the most reliable ways to predict how a finished garment will feel before you cut a single piece of cloth.

Getting comfortable with ease is one of those quiet turning points in a dressmaker's development. Once you understand that a pattern with 10cm of ease at the bust is not too big — it is intentionally relaxed — you stop second-guessing your size selection and start making more deliberate, confident choices.

Diagnosing What's Actually Wrong: Reading the Clues Your Fabric Leaves Behind

A poorly fitting garment is not a mystery — it is a message. The fabric tells you exactly what is wrong, if you know how to read it. Pulling, puckering, sagging, and twisting are not random; each one points to a specific structural mismatch between the pattern and your body. Learning to interpret these signals is one of the most valuable skills a dressmaker can develop, because it transforms a frustrating result into a solvable problem.

The Language of Drag Lines

Drag lines are diagonal pulls in the fabric, usually radiating outward from a point of tension. They appear when the fabric is being stretched across an area that is wider or fuller than the pattern allows for. A drag line pulling from the underarm towards the centre back, for instance, suggests the back is too narrow or the shoulders too wide for the pattern's proportions. Diagonal pulls across the front of a bodice from the bust point outward typically indicate that the bust cup is too small for your figure — the fabric is being dragged towards the fullest point because there is not enough room there.

The direction of a drag line almost always points towards the problem. If you follow the line back to where it originates, that is the area where the pattern needs more room.

Excess Fabric and Bagging

Where drag lines indicate too little room, bagging and excess fabric indicate too much. Horizontal folds below the back neck suggest the back length is too long for your torso. Vertical folds at the side seams of a skirt indicate the hip measurement is larger in the pattern than in your body. A drooping crotch seam on trousers suggests the rise — the distance from waist to crotch — is longer than needed.

Excess fabric is generally easier to correct than too little, since you are taking something in rather than adding something that is not there. Even so, it is worth identifying whether the excess is evenly distributed or concentrated in one area, as this affects how you approach the alteration.

Gaping and Pulling at Openings

A neckline that gapes away from the body, a zip that refuses to lie flat, or a waistband that pulls open at the centre front are all signs of tension between the pattern's proportions and your own. A gaping back zip on a dress is one of the most common complaints among home sewers, and it usually points to a difference between back waist length or back width rather than anything more complex. A neckline that gaps is often the result of a hollow chest or a difference in posture rather than an error in construction.

The Value of a Toile

This is the point at which the concept of a toile becomes not just useful but genuinely transformative. A toile — sometimes called a muslin, though the two terms are used interchangeably in the UK sewing community — is a test version of your garment made up in cheap fabric before you cut into anything precious. It allows you to try the pattern on your body, identify every fit issue, and make all your corrections on the toile before transferring them back to the paper pattern.

Making a toile feels like extra work, and in a sense it is. But consider the alternative: hours spent constructing a garment in your chosen fabric, only to discover at the final fitting that the shoulders sit two centimetres too wide, or that the waist falls at entirely the wrong point on your torso. A toile made from a few metres of calico or old sheeting costs very little and saves an enormous amount of frustration. For any garment with a complex fit — a tailored jacket, a fitted dress, a pair of trousers — it is simply the sensible approach.

Fit the toile as you would the finished garment, wearing the same underwear and shoes you intend to pair with it. Pin out any excess fabric, slash and spread where more room is needed, and mark all your adjustments clearly before unpicking the toile and using it to correct your pattern pieces. The time spent at this stage is never wasted.

The Alterations Every Home Sewer Should Have in Their Toolkit

Once you can diagnose a fit problem, the next question is how to fix it. Pattern alteration sounds technical, and some adjustments do require a degree of precision — but the majority of common fit issues can be resolved with a small set of techniques that, once learned, apply across almost every garment you will ever make. Think of these as the core vocabulary of fitting: once you know them, you can say almost anything.

Lengthening and Shortening

The most straightforward alteration, and the one most sewers need most often, is adjusting the length of a pattern piece. Most commercial patterns include printed lengthen/shorten lines — horizontal guidelines marked on the pattern piece specifically for this purpose. To shorten, you fold the pattern along the line, taking up the excess evenly. To lengthen, you cut along the line and spread the two pieces apart, inserting additional paper to fill the gap.

This adjustment is relevant far more broadly than just hemlines. Bodice back length, sleeve length, crotch rise on trousers, and the depth of a waistband can all be altered using the same principle. If your makes consistently pull up at the front or drop at the back, or if waistbands always sit at the wrong point on your torso, a lengthening or shortening adjustment is almost certainly what you need.

Grading Between Sizes

As mentioned earlier, it is extremely common for a person's measurements to fall into different pattern sizes at different points on the body. Someone with a fuller bust and narrower hips might need a size 18 at the chest and a size 14 at the hip. Rather than choosing one size and forcing the fit, the solution is to grade — to blend gradually between two sizes across the pattern piece. It is worth noting that the range of sewing patterns available in the UK makes this easier than ever, with many independent and commercial publishers now offering generous size ranges that span a wider spread of measurements on a single sheet, reducing the degree of grading required from the outset.

Grading is done by drawing a smooth, gradual line between the cutting lines of the two sizes, transitioning over the side seams or wherever the size difference needs to occur. The transition should be gentle enough that no sharp angles appear in the seam line. With a little practice it becomes intuitive, and it is one of the single most useful skills for achieving a well-fitted result from an unaltered pattern.

Full Bust Adjustment

The full bust adjustment, universally known among sewers as the FBA, deserves its own entry because it is both extremely common and frequently overlooked. Pattern companies typically draft bodices to a B cup. Anyone with a fuller bust than this will find that bodices pull across the chest, the underarm seam drifts forward, and buttons or zip plackets refuse to sit flat — all symptoms of a pattern that simply does not have enough room at the fullest point.

An FBA adds volume to the front bodice piece specifically at the bust, without affecting the shoulder seam, neckline, or overall length of the garment. It involves a series of cuts and pivots on the pattern piece that redistribute the fabric exactly where it is needed. It looks daunting the first time, but it follows a logical sequence, and the improvement in fit for those who need it is dramatic enough that most sewers who learn it wonder how they ever managed without it.

Sway Back Adjustment

The sway back adjustment is less well known but solves a problem that affects a significant number of sewers. A sway back is an inward curve of the lower spine more pronounced than the pattern's standard assumption, and its signature fit problem is a horizontal fold of excess fabric just above the centre back waist — the pattern is too long in the back bodice for your particular posture.

The fix is simple: take in a small horizontal tuck at the centre back, tapering to nothing at the side seams. This removes the excess length precisely where it is concentrated without altering anything else. It is a quick adjustment that makes an immediate and visible difference.

Shoulder Adjustments

Shoulders are one of the most critical fit points on any garment because they are structurally the anchor from which everything else hangs. If the shoulder seam sits too far forward or back, or if the slope of the seam does not match your own shoulder angle, nothing else in the garment will sit correctly no matter how many other adjustments you make.

Narrow shoulder adjustments reduce the width of the shoulder seam by taking in equal amounts at the armhole and the sleeve cap. Broad shoulder adjustments do the opposite. Adjusting for a steeper or shallower shoulder slope involves rotating the sleeve seam line to match your actual shoulder angle. These adjustments are worth prioritising because correcting them often resolves several other apparent fit issues simultaneously — what looks like a sleeve problem or a bust problem is frequently a shoulder problem in disguise.

Knowing When to Alter the Pattern and When to Adjust the Cloth

There is a distinction in dressmaking that does not get discussed nearly enough, and it is one that can save you significant time and frustration once you understand it. Not all fit adjustments are made at the same stage of the process, and choosing the right moment to intervene makes the difference between a correction that works cleanly and one that creates new problems whilst solving the original.

Alterations Made Before You Cut

The first and most thorough category of adjustment happens entirely on paper, before any fabric is touched. These are your pattern alterations — the lengthening, shortening, grading, and structural changes described in the previous section. Working at this stage gives you the most control, because you are working with flat pattern pieces that are easy to manipulate, measure, and compare.

If you have made the same garment before, or if you have made a toile and identified your adjustments clearly, this is where you make them permanent. Transfer your corrections to the pattern tissue or paper, true up any seam lines that have been affected, and check that corresponding pieces — the front and back bodice, for instance, or the sleeve and its armhole — still match correctly at their seams before you cut. A corrected pattern that has been properly trued up is an asset you can return to repeatedly, saving you from repeating the same diagnostic process every time you use it.

For sewers who make from a particular brand of sewing patterns regularly, building up a set of personalised, pre-altered pattern pieces is one of the most efficient things you can do. Your adjustments rarely change dramatically from project to project, and having them already incorporated means that every new make starts from a much more reliable foundation.

Fitting Adjustments on the Toile

The second stage of intervention is the toile fitting, which sits between the pattern work and the final construction. As covered earlier, a toile allows you to try the pattern on your body in three dimensions and catch anything that the flat-pattern alterations did not fully resolve — because however carefully you work on paper, the body is not flat, and some issues only reveal themselves when the fabric is actually worn.

At a toile fitting, you are looking for the residual problems: the slight pulling that remains after your FBA, the shoulder seam that still sits a few millimetres too far back, the waistline that falls just slightly above where you want it. These are the fine-tuning adjustments that elevate a garment from "fits well enough" to "fits properly." Mark them clearly on the toile with tailor's chalk or a fabric pen, unpick the relevant seams, measure the corrections, and transfer them back to your pattern before cutting the final fabric.

It is worth being systematic at this stage rather than trying to hold multiple adjustments in your head. Work from the top of the garment downwards — shoulders first, then bust, then waist, then hips — and deal with each area fully before moving to the next. Fit problems have a tendency to interact with one another, and resolving them in order prevents you from correcting something at the waist only to introduce a new problem at the hip.

In-Seam Corrections During Construction

The third stage is the one most sewers rely on exclusively, and whilst it is a legitimate tool, it works best as a final refinement rather than a primary strategy. In-seam corrections are adjustments made during the construction of the actual garment — taking in or letting out seam allowances, adjusting dart placement, or easing a seam to encourage the fabric to lie correctly.

The advantage of in-seam corrections is their immediacy. You can try the garment on at each stage of construction, assess what needs adjusting, and make small changes in real time. Many experienced sewers develop an instinct for this, making tiny adjustments almost without thinking as they sew.

The limitation is that in-seam corrections only work within narrow margins. If a bodice is significantly too wide at the back, you can take in the side seams — but only up to the point where doing so starts to distort the armhole or pull the front of the garment out of position. In-seam adjustments cannot add fabric that is not there, and they cannot move structural elements like darts or seamlines without affecting the overall shape of the garment. For significant fit issues, they are a finishing touch, not a solution.

The most effective approach is to think of these three stages as layers rather than alternatives. Pattern alterations establish the correct proportions on paper. The toile fitting catches what the paper work missed. In-seam corrections refine the final result. Used together, they give you complete control over how a finished garment fits — and that control, more than any other single factor, is what separates a handmade garment that looks truly made to measure from one that simply looks homemade.

The Best-Fitting Garment You'll Ever Wear Is the One You Make Yourself

There is a quiet irony at the heart of home dressmaking. The very thing that puts so many people off — the complexity of fit, the adjustments, the toiles, the technical language — is also the thing that makes it extraordinary. No high-street retailer will ever draft a pattern to your specific back length, your particular shoulder slope, or the exact point at which your waist sits. No mass-produced garment will ever account for the full combination of proportions that make your body yours. But you can. That is the remarkable thing.

The path to a well-fitted handmade garment is not a straight line, and it would be dishonest to suggest otherwise. There will be toiles that reveal more problems than you expected, alterations that need to be unpicked and redone, and finished garments that are almost right but not quite. This is not failure — it is the process. Every correction you make teaches you something about your body and about the relationship between flat pattern pieces and three-dimensional form. That knowledge accumulates, and it compounds. The fifth garment you make from a pattern you have properly adjusted will fit immeasurably better than the first, with a fraction of the diagnostic work.

The practical takeaway from everything covered in this guide is straightforward: measure accurately and do it regularly, always compare your measurements to the pattern's size chart rather than guessing, learn to read what your fabric is telling you, and build the habit of making a toile for any garment where fit genuinely matters. These are not advanced techniques reserved for experienced sewers — they are foundational habits that will transform your results from the very beginning.

The alterations themselves — grading between sizes, adjusting for shoulder width, making a full bust adjustment, correcting a sway back — are skills that take a little time to learn and a lifetime to refine. But each one, once understood, becomes part of a permanent toolkit that travels with you across every project you ever make. You will not need all of them every time. But knowing they exist, and knowing how to use them, means that a poorly fitting toile is never the end of the story.

So the next time you try on a finished garment and find it pulling across the shoulders or bagging at the waist, resist the urge to blame yourself or the pattern. Instead, ask the more useful question: what is the fabric trying to tell me, and what do I need to do about it? The answer is almost always within reach — and the garment waiting on the other side of that answer will fit better than anything you could ever buy.
